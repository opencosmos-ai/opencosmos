# 艮 — `mountain`

**The rendering is the image, not the action.** 說卦 gives every trigram two glosses: an action
in chapter 7 (艮，…也) and an image in chapter 11 (艮為山). The 大象傳 bound into
every hexagram operates on the **images** — across the 64 they name 天 18 times, 地 17, 雷 15,
山 15, 澤 15, 水 11, 風 10, 火 10, against 健 once, 順 twice, 入 twice, 麗 once, and 動 陷 止 說
not at all. Rendering by image is not promoting one Wing's gloss over another; it is the reading
the vendored text runs on. See [`../WORKLIST.md`](../WORKLIST.md) § A1.

**山 is mountain, in 15 of the 64 大象傳** — 山下出泉 (蒙), 山上有木 (漸), 山附於地 (剝) —
and McClatchie writes Mountain or Mountains 15 times. No lock, no split, no live alternative.

**Singular, not plural.** McClatchie writes *Mountains* 14 times against *Mountain* once, which
is a nineteenth-century habit rather than a reading of 山. The 大象傳 stack one tier on another,
and a plural reads as a range rather than a tier.

**The seam.** 說卦 ch 7 glosses 艮 止 — stopping, coming to rest — and it is the trigram's whole
sense in 52 艮 itself, where it doubles and the judgment is about stillness. Mountain is the
image and carries the stillness implicitly, which is the best case available: the reader who
sees a mountain is not far from the reader who sees stopping.

## What is ruled out, and why

- **"Keeping Still", "stillness", "the immovable"** — the action (止) rather than the image. They
  break every stacking: 山上有木 (漸) is wood on the mountain, not wood on a stillness. The
  stopping is carried implicitly — a reader who sees a mountain is not far from one who sees
  stopping — and explicitly in the notes at 52 艮.
