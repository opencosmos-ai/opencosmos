# Nonviolence — Cross-Tradition Synthesis

## Summary

Nonviolence (*ahimsa* in Sanskrit, *satyagraha* in Gandhi's coinage) is not a single position but a spectrum — from Tolstoy's absolute refusal of all violence to Thoreau's targeted noncooperation to Buddhist non-harm as an ethical precept. What unites the spectrum: violence generates more violence; suffering accepted without retaliation has moral and political force that violence lacks; and the inner state of the actor — conscience, intention, love — matters as much as the external action. What divides it: whether nonviolence is an absolute moral law, a strategic choice, a spiritual practice, or a practical political method.

**The sharpest disagreement in the corpus is not between the nonviolent traditions and a Vedic counter-tradition. It is inside the Vedic tradition itself.** Patañjali's *Yoga Sutras* make *ahimsa* the first of the five abstentions and then, in II.31, refuse to qualify it "by species or place or time or exigency" — an exceptionless formulation that names and rejects the very exemptions the *Bhagavad-Gita* relies on when Krishna overrides Arjuna's refusal to fight. Two classical Vedic texts, ruling against each other on the corpus's hardest ethical question.

## Key Claims

- **Patañjali's exceptionless ahimsa:** Non-injury is the first of the five *yamas*, the foundation of the eight-limbed system — nothing else in the practice is reachable without it. Woods renders II.30: "Abstinence from injury and from falsehood and from theft and from incontinence and from acceptance of gifts are the abstentions." The decisive move is **II.31**: "When they are unqualified by species or place or time or exigency and when [covering] all [these] classes — there is the Great Course-of-conduct" (*mahāvratam*). Each of those four qualifiers is an exemption someone has claimed; the sutra refuses all four at once.
- **Ahimsa extends to complicity:** II.34 counts injuries "whether done or caused to be done or approved, whether ensuing upon greed or anger or infatuation, whether mild or moderate or vehement." Approval is culpable. This is structurally the argument Tolstoy makes about taxation, military service and policing — that the citizen who consents is implicated — arriving roughly seventeen centuries earlier and from karmic rather than Christian premises.
- **The fruit of ahimsa is described as social, not merely personal:** II.35, "As soon as he is grounded in abstinence from injury, his presence begets a suspension of enmity" (Johnston: "Where non-injury is perfected, all enmity ceases in the presence of him who possesses it"). Nonviolence is claimed to disarm the surrounding field, not only to keep the practitioner clean — the same claim Gandhi would later make operationally.
- **Buddhist ahimsa as foundational precept:** Non-harm toward all sentient beings is one of the first precepts of Buddhist ethics. The *Dhammapada*: "He who, seeking his own happiness, punishes or kills beings who also long for happiness, will not find happiness after death." The ground: all beings share the capacity for suffering; causing unnecessary suffering is a violation of that shared nature.
- **Tolstoy's absolute nonresistance:** The Sermon on the Mount's "Do not resist evil by force" is a literal command for all Christian life. All state violence — military service, policing, capital punishment — is complicity in evil. The only principled response is universal conscientious refusal. (*Kingdom of God Is Within You*)
- **Thoreau's targeted noncooperation:** Resistance to specific injustices through withdrawal of participation — refuse to pay the tax, accept the jail cell, make the injustice visible through principled suffering. This is not pacifism as such but nonviolent resistance to a specific wrong. (*Civil Disobedience*)
- **Quaker peace testimony:** "There is that of God in every man" — therefore killing any person is an act of sacrilege, regardless of the legal or political context. George Fox's Inner Light theology generates a peace testimony that predates both Thoreau and Tolstoy by 200 years.
- **The Gandhian synthesis:** Gandhi explicitly acknowledged Thoreau's *Civil Disobedience* and Tolstoy's *Kingdom of God Is Within You* as direct influences. His synthesis: targeted noncooperation (Thoreau) + absolute principled nonviolence (Tolstoy) + Hindu karma yoga (action without attachment to outcome) + willing acceptance of suffering as a moral and political instrument. That he could draw on Hindu sources for *both* halves — the Gita for action, the yogic *yamas* for ahimsa — is a fact this page previously obscured.

## Contradictions

- **The Vedic tradition contradicts itself, and this is the page's central finding.** Patañjali II.31 makes ahimsa unqualified by species, place, time or exigency; the Gita exempts Arjuna on exactly those grounds. Framing the Gita as "the counter-tradition to nonviolence" — as this page did before 2026-09-14 — mistook one voice in an internal argument for the whole tradition's position.
- Buddhism teaches non-harm as a universal precept, but early Buddhist history includes Ashoka (who converted after the bloody Kalinga war) rather than prohibiting all political violence in advance. The tradition has historically found ways to accommodate just-war reasoning that Tolstoy explicitly rejects.
- Thoreau and Tolstoy both invoke conscience as the supreme authority — but they arrive at different practical positions (targeted vs. absolute noncooperation) from this shared premise. Whose conscience is right? They can't both be reading the same inner authority.
- Patañjali's ahimsa is grounded in the practitioner's liberation — injury binds the one who commits it — whereas Tolstoy's and Fox's are grounded in the standing of the victim (a soul that must not be resisted, that of God in every person). The behaviour converges; the reasons do not, and the difference shows under pressure: a self-liberation ethic has a harder time explaining intervention on behalf of a third party.

## Open Questions

- Is nonviolence an absolute moral law (Tolstoy, Patañjali), a strategic choice (Gandhi's later formulation), a spiritual practice (Buddhist ahimsa, the yamas as prerequisite), or a situational ethical judgment (the Gita)? These are not the same claim.
- The corpus now holds both Vedic positions but no text that *adjudicates* between them. Is there a classical source that addresses the Gita and the Yoga Sutras together on this point, and should it be curated?
- Does Patañjali's placement of ahimsa *first* among the yamas — as the precondition for everything downstream in the eight limbs — constitute an argument, or only an ordering?
